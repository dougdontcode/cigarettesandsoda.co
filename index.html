Nine Years Now, Mastodon Stubbornly Survives

Oct 11, 2026

I was really bored on a Sunday and stumbled upon this article while blundering around Micro.ink. The author, Michał “rysiek” Woźniak, shows some surprise that Mastodon—the uppity, federated social network—is not only still going, but it’s growing nine years after its inception.

I remember when Mastodon started to take off. I had been a member of the kinda snooty new, ad-free social network you _pay_to join.

App.net was the network back in 2012, where tech geeks and hardcore coders joined to chat and bitch about how Twitter was a cesspool (still is) and how Facebook’s main agenda was propagating as much personal data as it could. Google was still the big boogeyman of privacy and security.

App.net was a haven from the roaring idiocracy most social media was becoming. I mean, what kind of idiot would pay $10 a month to do what could be done for free on Twitter? Tech oligarchs.

App.net was one of the first federated social networks, meaning that it was:

A group of computing or network providers agreeing upon standards of operation in a collective fashion…meaning) to be federated…users are able to send messages from one network to the other without being interconnected.

App.net was the precursor to Mastodon’s larger swarm of networks. Twitter ran on a centralized network where all posts and media were stored on centralized servers. App.net waltzed in and distributed its network across multiple, unconnected servers.

For me, the loan arts-obsessed Humanities major in a throng of tech-fiends actually found a lot of comfort in my little instance. I learned how to code Python from a user named after Doctor Who. I taught an aspiring author how to organize and write a novel. My life was saved because someone was there one night when I was at my worst.

App.net folded in 2017, and its users were left to shamble back to Twitter, join up on esoteric, niche services, and lose all of the friends they’d made on App.net.

Along came Mastodon. It offered us refugees of App.net a new social network where we could reconnect with each other and share our stupid anecdotes once again. The best thing about Mastodon was that a user with an account on one instance—https://mastodon.social—could follow their friend who signed up on Https://hackers.town, who could then follow another friend signed up on https://mastodon.lol. The single point of signup, and failure of most of the other major social networks, was replaced by a swarm of instances all running under the same protocol but on totally separate sites.

App.net turned into Appdot.net. The techlords saw to it that we refugees would be back together with a hint of nostalgia.

I’m nowhere near technical enough to spin up my own Mastodon instance, but I’ve heard it’s easy. A domain, the shared protocol, and a stable enough server were all that was needed. Even better, instead of putting the burden of blocking hateful users, whole instances and their users could be blocked entirely from the majority of Mastodon instances if they became a breeding ground of hate and fascist ideology.

I drifted onto App.net in 2014 and have only seen growth. And I was reunited with my old friends, whom I still annoy to this day.

As Michal Woźniak says in his post:

Turns out what drives and sustains decentralized social media is not hype, but a sense of community and the possibility to have some real agency in one’s social media experience. This decentralization and sense of community allow Fediverse to survive even if individual instances disappear.

The weak point of the major social media companies is centralization. Only one domain, only one protocol, only one user experience and user interface, and, most importantly, only one large user base.

What keeps Mastodon chugging on, even when all the tech blogs were predicting its doom, is the communal aspects of the social network. There are multiple clients that can connect to your account; each instance has its own rules and ideologies; a user doesn’t have to fragment their social experience across multiple accounts. One federated account gets you access to tons of other services like Micro.blog and Instagram substitute Pixelfed.

There were some justified issues with the complex onboarding process of Mastodon. In the early days, creating an account and finding people to follow was quite complicated. This turned many people off. But it’s become easier, and the Fediverse continues to grow.

Michal Woźniak once again points out:

Google+, which despite corporate backing, an incomparably larger budget, and being pushed down people’s throats through forced integration with YouTube, survived a mere seven years, nine months, and a week.

And that:

once it shut down, it shut down. A social network of millions just blipped out of existence one day. All those moments – lost in time, like tears in rain.

If Facebook were to shut down suddenly, billions of creations would be lost. If Instagram pulls the plug tomorrow, so many masterpieces will just disappear forever in a blip. Millions of activists on Twitter (I refuse to call it X) would lose their platform if the CEO of the company threw a tantrum and burned it to the ground.

Not with Mastodon. If an instance shuts down for some reason, the data stored on its servers is transferred to another instance, and the community is back up in minutes. There is no cackling overlord who bans on serendipity.

So, 9 years ago, the tech community was all grumbling about Mastodon being a fad and would dissipate quickly. Instead, it’s still going strong and has introduced the mass culture to the idea of Federation.

Keep on tooting friends.

How Music Influenced Totally Fu##d Up My Writing

Oct 11, 2026

Sorry if this post is all wonky in formatting and shit. I’m still learning my workflow for Ulysses to Micro.blog to multiple shares

I’m a huge music and film lover. So much so, I wrote my graduate thesis on the topics I used to write music reviews for Popmatters.com back when it was a small internet magazine. My favorite concert is a bit different than most of the music I like. I’m an old Punk. Not a serious punk with tons of tattoos, piercings, or other body modifications. But I do drift towards anarchy, and Nazis always need to be punched.

I write Cyberpunk horror fiction. I know, another small dish in a huge ocean. But I’ve been published, which means a lot to me.

I base my stories around the music I’m listening to. A short story that won me $100 in a contest began with an experience I had while smoking in an alley outside a Soul Coughing concert. The idea of identity and hiding behind cigarettes, booze, or self-deprecation fascinated me, especially when exposed in neon-glowing light muffled by cigarette smoke in an alley echoing with noise from the street and the thump of the bands.

I’m really a fan of post-rock from Mogwai, Explosions in the Sky, Godspeed You! Black Emperor (find them on Bandcamp!), maybeshewill, 65daysofstatic, and other crescendo-core, cinematic types of bands.

Oh! And also The End of the Ocean, Russian Circles, Bruit ≤ (only available on Bandcamp; all albums are free for streaming but it’s soworth it), This Will Destroy You, and recently I discovered Overhead, the Albatross

Portishead’s “Third” influenced my only novel. As did maybeshewill’s: “Red Paper Lanterns” and “Not For Want of Trying.”

I fell in love with someone who exposed massive holes in my Punk Rock library. While my High School peers were skating to NoFx’s “Linoleum” and Lagwagon’s “Violins”, I was embarrassingly spinning Christian “rock”:

youtu.be/ZAvo3Q3ed…

Umm, I can barely even remember the names: Carmen, a bafflingly good singer busting out generic rock and white-guy rap for Jesus; DC Talk, a horrible (and I think only) Christian “rap” group that was passed around in churches. Amy Grant before she went vanilla generic easy-listening. Just bad, bad music. And bad literature from Frank Perriti, the Stephen King of the Christian horror genre: he wrote only about spiritual warfare and Second Coming nonsense.

Thankfully, my older sister went off to college and left me her collection of Depeche Mode, The Pixies), Fishbone, David Bowie, Sex Pistols, The Cure, and Prince tapes (to name a few.)

My mind was blown. I drifted into the underground in the 90s, but not too far: Soul Coughing, Ashley MacIsaac, Violent Femmes, but finally found myself when I went to college and got into mid-90s Midwest Emo, more defined by a pop-punk sound than the morphing Emo of the 2000s characterized by a the more Goth ethos shown in My Chemical Romance, Thursday, and the Alkaline Trio.

Modest Mouse, Low, and, for no reason other than I was hooked on the smarmy Swing music revival in the early to mid-2000swith Hot Jazz ensemble Squirrel Nut Zipperswith the break out hit “Hell” and punk infused rock-a-billy wannabes Cherry Poppin’ Daddiesand their catchy late 1997 weird “Zoot Suit Riot”. I annoyed everyone in my dorm by blasting hipsters cosplaying idiotic Swing bands right before the coming millennium. I wonder what the hell we were thinking during that summer.

At the same time, in the late 90s-early 2000s, I found my passion for writing. The two major influences on my developing life greatly affected others. I listened to Mineral(who just happened to have a huge impact on the loud-sod-loud dynamic of future indie rock bands) when lonely and longing, Cap’n Jazz to try to earn my underground indie cred, and the album “Clarity” by Jimmy Eat Worldholding fast as my warm, comforting blanket, Emo was more for a yearning for future hopes and young me dreams than goth tinged whining about girls.

In fact, I remember buying Jimmy Eat World’s albumBleed American on 9/11/2001. When it was still named “Bleed American.” The band changed the album’s name out of respect but reverted to the original title much later. Over 20 years later and My Sundown still hits me like a shockwave.

I was teaching undergrad classes at the time. I remember specifically thatThe Middlewas pounding out of my cheap stereo right at the exact time the second plane ran into the tower.

I immediately cancelled all classes that day. “Bleed American” became the soundtrack of that day, which was a weird juxtaposition.

I was 21.

Now I’m 48 and am just rediscovering the amazing sounds of NoFx (especially prescient today with the best punk rock song ever written, The Decline. I also played the Dead Kennedy’sFuck Off Nazi Punkson endless repeat during the second Bush administration and, even louder now, during the hellish Trump administrations. Fuck Donald Trump and Fuck MAGA.

So this has been my long, rambling bio about how my writing wouldn’t exist without the music that inspired it. Many thanks, also, to the literary geniuses of William Gibson https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gibson,Thomas Pynchon https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Pynchon, Margaret Atwood https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Atwood,Mark Z. Danielewski for House of Leaves, , and the inspiration and influence from my great Folklore professor and graduate advisor Rosemary Hathaway.

It’s been a long decade since I’ve written anything meaningful. I’ve been listening to so much music through these years, though, from Taylor Swift to Nine Inch Nails to Propagandhi to the amazing anger from Dead Pioneers , raging guitars over dead treaties, stolen land, cultural appropriation, and genocide.
